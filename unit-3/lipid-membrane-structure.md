Q: What do structural lipids & storage lipids have in common?
A: Both contain fatty acids, water-insoluble hydrocarbon chains

Q: What are Triacylglycerols (or triglycerides)
A: Storage lipids, stored for energy in adipocytes. Glycerol backbone + 3 fatty acid chains.

Fatty acid esters of glycerol; composed of 3 fatty acids each in ester linkage with a single glycerol. Non-polar, hydrophobic. Can be simple (one kind of fatty acid) or mixed (2-3 different fatty acids)

Vertebrates store triacylglycerols as lipid droplets in adipocytes (fat cells). Plants store in seeds.

Q: What are glycerophospholipids?
A: Amphipathic membrane lipids composed of hydrophobic tails attached to polar head groups. Have glycerol or sphingosine backbones.

3 types:
- Phospholipids: 
    - Glycerophospholipids (glycerol backbone + 2 fatty acid + a phosphate & head group)
    - Sphingolipids (sphingosine background, 1 fatty acid, 1 phosphate & head group)
- Glycolipids
    - Glycosphingolipids (sphingosine backbone, 1 fatty acid, 1 sugar (mono or oligosaccharide)
    - Galactolipids (sulfolipids) (Glycerol, 2 fatty acid, sugar + so4)
- Sterols
    - Four ring steroid, 1 fatty acid, 1 polar head group. Cholesterol example, can be part of bilayer

Q: What are biological membranes made of?
A: Phospholipid bilayer + proteins & steroids

Q: Effects of hydrogenation
A: Oils are more unsaturated whereas solid is more saturated. Natural fats are mixtures of simple and mixed triacylglycrols with varying chain lenghts and degrees of saturation.

Q: What is partial hydrogenation?
A: Harmful manufactured fats where cis double bonds are converted to single bonds (hydrogenating them) improves shelf life & stability but some cis double bonds are converted to trans double bonds which are unhealthful. Being phased out or banned in many countries.

Q: What is phospholipid bilayer?
A: Biological membranes have a double layer of lipids that acts as a barrier to polar molecules and ions. Membrane lipids are ampipathic (one end hydrophobic, one philic) - hydrophobic regions associate with each other and hydrophilic regions associate with water.

Q: What are glycerophospholipids?
A: Glycerophospholipids (or phosphoglycerides or just phospholipids) are membrane lipids in which two fatty acid are attached in ester linkage to the first and second carbons of glycerol and a highly polar or charged group is attached through a phosphodiester linkage to the third carbon. In general glyceroiphospholipids contain a C16 or C17 saturated fatty acid at C-1 and a C18 or C20 unsaturated fatty acid at C-2.

Q: Lipid bilayer
A: Glycerophospholipids are virtually insoluble in water. Sp;ontaneously form microscopic lipid aggregates when mixed with water. Hydrophobic surfaces of molecule cluster in an aqueous environment to find the lowest energy environment by reducing the hydrophobic surface area exposed to water.

- Micelles are spherical structures containing amphipathic molecules arranged with hydrophobic regions in the interior and hydrophilic head groups on the exterior
- Bilayer is a lipid aggregate in which two lipid monolayers (leaflets) form a 2d sheet
- A vesicle or liposome forms spontaneously when a bilayer sheet folds back on itself to form a hollow sphere

Q: Fatty acid melting points:
A: At RT saturated fatty acids have waxy consistency and unsaturated fatty acids are oily liquids. Extent of packing depends on degree of saturation. Saturated fatty acids pack closely while mixtures of saturated and unsaturated fatty acids creates kinks and don't pack as closely. Saturated fatty acids have van der waals forces between them.

Q: How are plycerophospholipids named?
A: As derivatives of phosphatidic acid. A phosphodiester bond joins the head group to glycerol. The phosphate group can bear a negative, neutral, or positive charge.

Q: How does lipid composition of membranes differ?
A: The functional specialization of each membrane type is reflected in its unique lipid composition.

Q: What are ceramides?
A: Structural parent of all sphingolipids. C-1, C-2, and C-3 of sphingosine are structurally analogous of the three carbons of glycedrol in glycerophospholipids.

Glycosphingolipids occur largely in the outer face of plasma membranes.

Q: What are sphingolipids?
A: Large class of membrane phospholipids and glycolipids. Power head group and two nonpolar tail. No glycerol, contain one molecule of the longchain amino alcohol sphingosine or one of its derivatives.

Q: Gangliosides
A: Often found in neurons; oligioosaccharides as polar head gropups and 1+ resiues of N-acetylneuraminic acid, a sialic acid, at the termini play roles in neuronal stuff. Involved in neurotransmission, synaptic plasticity, and formation of memory.

Q: Membrane sphingolipids 
A: Membrane sphingolipids serve as sources of intracellular messengers. Ceramides and sphingomyelin are regulators of cellular signaling.

Involved in cell division, differentiation, migration, cell death. FOrm lipid rafts.

Q: Membrane functions
A: shape changes that accompany cell growth and movement. Exocytosis, endocytosis, cell division. Molecular gatekeepers.

Q: Types of membrane protein
A: Transporters, receptors, ion channels, adhesion molecules.

Q: What is fluid mosaic model
A: COnstantly moving arrangement of molecules in phospholipid bilayer. Proteins can be anchored, transmembrane.

Q: Endomembrane system
A: Single membrane surrounds ER, golgi, lysosomes, various small vesicles.

Double layer in nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts.

Q: What is glycosylation?
A: Attachment of oligosaccharides to proteins typically on the otuer face of plasma membrane.

Q: Topology of integral membrane protein can be predicted from sequence
A: a helix of 20-25 residues is just long enough to span lipid bilayer (30A). Stabilized by intrachain h bonding and hydrophobic effect.

Hydropathy index expresses free-energy change associated with the movement of an amino acid side chian from a hydrophobic environment to water. Ranges from highly exergonic to highly endergonic. Can be plotted - number of hydrophobic regions will predict how many transmembrane domains.

Q: Ordered and disordered states
A: 2 states, liquid ordered (Lo) and liquid disordered (Ld) acyl groups in bilayer interior. Highly constrained in Lo, have lateral and rotation motion in Ld. At physiological temperatures long-chian satuirated fatty acids pack into Lo; kinks in unsaturated fatty acids favor Ld; shorter-chain fatty acyl groups favor Ld. Heat produyces thermal motion of side chains and promotes Lo -> Ld. Membranes fall inbetween.

Q: How does setrol content affect membrane fluidity?
A: Paradoxical effect - keeps saturated fatty acid tails from packing too tightly increading fluidity at low temp. Stabilizes membrane structure (decreasing fluidity) at high temps. Tempers phase transitions.

Q: Latral diffusion in bilayer
A: FRAP (flurorescent recovery after photobleaching) - tag cell surface with fluorescents, bleach area with high power laser, can see unbleached phospholipids diffusing into the bleached area.

Q: Hop diffusion
A: Single phospholpid tracking confirms lipid molecules diffuse laterally within small regions. Movement from one region to another ("hop diffusion") is rarer.

Caused by large aggregates or anchors to internal structures.

Q: Inner vs outer leaflet
A: Almost all phosphatidyl serine is on inner. PE inner, PI, PIPs inner. PCholine outer, SphingoMyelin outside, GSLs outside.

Q: Transbilayer movement of lipids
A: Flippases catalyze translocation of PEthanolamine and PSerine from extracellular to cytoplasmic leaflet. Takes ATP

Floppases move phospholpids from cytosol to outer leaflet. Takes ATP

Scramblases move lipids in either direction towards equilibrium. No ATP

Q: Membrane rafts
A: Sphingolipids and cholesterol cluster in membrane rafts. Microdomains that have tendency to stay together. Surrouinded by neighboring phospholipid-rich regions. More ordered with specific functions.

Q: Caveolin
A: Integral protein that binds inner leaflet. Dimer forces bilayer to curve inwards to form caveolae. Can increase or decrease surface area and bud off vessicles.
