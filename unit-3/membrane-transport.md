Q: Simple diffusion
A: Nonpolar compounds can move down concentration gradients through bilayer

Movement of solute from higher to lower concentration. No transporters needed

Q: Facilitated diffusion
A: Down electrochemical gradient; moves through a channel

Substrate moves across membrane with the concentration gradient, needs a channel to allow nonpolar stuff through.

Q: Primary active transport
A: Against electrochemical gradient, driven by ATP

Must go against gradient, pumping something into or out of cell. Thermodynamically unfavorable & must be coupled to an exergonic process to happen.

Primary active transporters use energy provided directly by a chemical reaction. Solute accumulation is coupled directly to an exergonic chemical reaction.

Q: Secondary active transport
A: Against electrochemical gradient, driven by ions moving down its gradient.

Couple uphill transport of one substrate with downhill transport of another. Endergonic transport of one solute is coupled to the exergonic flow of a different solute that was originally pumped uphill by primary active transport.

Using primary active transport to create an ion gradient provides energy to perform secondary active tgransport of another molecule such as an ion, a sugar, or an amino acid.

Q: 3 general classes of transport systems
A: Anitport: two solutes move in opposite directions

Symport: two solutes move in same direction

Uniport: only carry one substrate

Q: Ion channel
A: Down electrochemical gradient; may be gated by ligand or ion

Selective; provide an aqueous path across membrane through which inorganic ions can diffuse at high rates.

- Most have a "gate" regulated by a biological signal
- Typically show specificity for an ion
- Are not saturatable with their ion substrate
- Flow stops either when the gate is closed or when there is no longer an electrochemical gradient
- The rate of flux can be orders of magnitude greater than the turnover for a transporter

Q: How does K+ channel ion channel specificity work?
A: The channel stabilizes the specific ion as it passes through (other ions are not allowed). Negatively charged amino acid residues at the interface increase local concentrations of positively charged ions. Dipoles present inside channel. Right size for K+ but not Na+. 2 K+ come in at once; first occupy positions 1 and 3 then 2 and 4. Backbone carbonyl oxygens form cage that fits K+ perfectgly, replacing water molecules of ions hydration sphere.

Q: Membrane potential (Vm)
A: Transmembrane electrical gradient that occurs when ions of opposite charges are separated by a membrane. Produces a force that opposes ion movements that incrase Vm, drives movements that reduce Vm.

Q: Ligand gated ion channels in the brain
A: When a presynaptic neuron is excited it releases neurotransmitters into synaptic cleft. Binding of ligand to postsynaptic neuron causes an allosteric change in the protein which opens or closes the channel.

Allows Na+, K+, Ca2+, and/or Cl- to pass through (specific for one ion). Results in a depolarization for an excitatory receptor response or a hyperpolarization for an inhibitory response.

Q: Voltage gated ion channels
A: Activated by changes in a cells electrical membrane potential near the channel. Alters the conformation of the channel proteins, regulating opening and closing.

In Neurons this allows depolarization in response to a voltage change. Allows ions through (Na+, K+, Ca2+, and/or Cl-). Opening and closing triggered by changing ion concentrations and thus charge gradient between sides of cell membrane.

3 discrete units: Voltage sensor (in transmembrane domains), pore or conducting pathway, and the gate. There may also be an inactivation gate to differentiate resting state from an inactive state.

Q: Ion channels vs transporters
A: Gated ion channels have specificity for an ion. When open, ions flow freely and rapidly with no saturation. Stops when gate closes.

Transporters have specificity for their substrates. Rate is limited because they can be saturated. Can go either with or against gradient. Gates around pore alternate opening and closing to allow transport. Typically only one is open at a time.

Q: Chloride-bicarbonate exchanger
A: Passive transport; antiport exchanger.

Waste CO2 from respiring tissues enters blood and is taken up by erythrocytes where it is converted into bicarbonate (HCO3-) by carbonic anhydrase. Then it reenters blood where it is soluble. Nearing the lungs, it reenters erythrocytes and is converted back to CO2 to be exhaled.

Passive anion exchanger essential in CO2 transport to lungs from tissues. For every HCO3- that moves in one direction, a Cl- moves in the other direction. There is no net change in charge.

Q: Na+ K+ ATPase in animal cells
A: Moves Na+ and K+ against their electrochemical gradients to maintain low [Na+] and high [K+] in the cell (essential to conduction of action potentials in neurons).

- After binding ATP it binds 3 intracellular Na+ ions
- ATP hydrolyzed, leading to phosphorylation of pump at a highly conserved Asp residue & release of ADP. Conformational change in pump.
- Conformational change exposes Na+ ion to the extracellular region and they are released.
- Pump then binds 2 extracellular K+ ions which induces dephosphorylation of the pump, reverting it to its previous conformational state, thus releasing the K+ ions into the cell.

Q: ABC transporters use ATP to drive active transport
A: Family of ATP-driven transporters that pump substrates across a membrane against a concentration gradient. Have 2 ATP binding domains and 2 transmembrane domains. Mostly plasma membrane, but also ER, mitochondria, lysosomes.

Substrates move across the membrane when two forms of the transporter interconvert. This is driven by ATP hydrolysis. Switches between inward facing and outward facing to move compounds.

Q: How is glucose moved from intestine to bloodstream?
A: Na+-glucose symporter takes up glucose from intestine on the apical membrane side, in a process driven by downhill flow of Na+ (strong thermodynamic tendency for Na+ to move into cell). Provides energy needed for transport of glucose into cell against concentration gradient. On the basal side, a glucose uniporter GLUT2 moves glucose out of the cell into bloodstream while a Na+K+ ATPase moves Na+ into bloodstream and K+ into cell.

Insulin increases transport of GLUT4 receptors to cell surface, bringing glucose into the cell.

Q: Aquaporins
A: Aquaporins form hydrophilic transmembrane channels for the passage of water. Saliva, sweat, tears all use aquaporins. Also important in kidney for water resorption. Each protein has a specific location and role. Does not allow passage of protons. Targeted by diuretics.
